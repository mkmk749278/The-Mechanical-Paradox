## Cold open
A one-hundred-ton locomotive has no steering wheel, no differential gears, and a solid steel axle. Yet it rounds curves at high speed, and its wheels stay on the rails.

Here's the catch. Take a bend that turns the track through a right angle. The outer rail is about two point four meters longer than the inner one. Two wheels, locked to one axle, turning at the same speed. So how does one travel farther?

One wheel should skid and scream. But on a gentle curve, neither one really slips.

The answer is in the shape of the wheel. And that same shape explains the screech you hear on a tight bend, and why fast trains sway.

## Why the obvious answers fail
Your car has a differential, a box of gears that lets the outside wheel spin faster. A train has nothing like it. Both wheels are fixed to one axle, so they always turn together.

So do the flanges steer it? Those steel lips on the inside of each wheel, pressing on the rail? That is what most of us would guess. But on a gentle curve, the flanges do not need to touch the rail at all.

## The cone
Look closely at the wheel, and you will see it. The surface it rolls on is not flat at all. It is a cone, sloping about one in twenty, so the wheel gets slightly bigger toward the flange.

Now watch what happens in a curve. The whole axle shifts a few millimeters toward the outside of the bend. The outer wheel now rolls on a bigger circle, and the inner wheel rolls on a smaller one. Same spin, different distance.

It shifts until those two rolling circles form a cone of their own, a cone whose tip points at the center of the curve. And the axle steers itself round the bend.

So how big is the difference? On a textbook wheelset, shifted as far as it can go, the two wheels differ in radius by about seven tenths of a millimeter. Thinner than the bank card in your wallet.

That is the entire steering system. No moving parts. Just geometry.

It even works on straight track. Drift to the left, and the left wheel gets bigger, so the axle turns back to the right. It is always pulling itself toward the middle.

## Where the cone runs out
But geometry has a limit. Imagine you are on a curve with a radius of five hundred meters. To roll round it cleanly, that textbook wheelset would have to shift fifteen millimeters outward. It only has about seven before the flange meets the rail.

So a one in twenty cone can only roll smoothly round curves wider than about a kilometer in radius. Any tighter, and it runs out of cone.

So what happens when you take a tight curve? The wheels slip. The flange presses against the rail. The axle is dragged round at a slight angle, so the tread is forced sideways across the top of the rail.

And that is the screech. You would think it is the flange rubbing. But most researchers say it is the tread, sliding sideways, sticking and slipping so fast that the whole wheel rings like a bell.

The flange has a limit of its own. More than a century ago, an engineer named Nadal worked out how hard a flange can be pushed sideways before it climbs the rail. For a typical flange, the sideways push can grow to about the same as the weight pressing down on that wheel. Go past it, and the wheel can ride up and over the rail. That is one way a train comes off the track.

## The price of the cone
And here is the twist. The cone that keeps a train centered also makes it sway.

Push the axle to one side, and the cone steers it back. But does it stop in the middle? No. It overshoots, and steers back again. The axle snakes along the track in a smooth wave. Railway engineers call it hunting.

Engineers knew about this sway two hundred years ago. In eighteen eighty-three, an engineer named Klingel worked out the length of that wave. For our textbook wheelset, about seventeen meters. At a hundred and sixty kilometers an hour, that is about two and a half sways every second.

At low speed, the sway is damped out. But above a certain speed, the critical speed, it feeds on itself, and it grows until the flanges hammer the rails.

So fast trains use flatter cones. Japan's bullet trains used wheels with a slope of one in forty, half as steep. And stiff suspension holds the axles straight.

See the trade-off? A steeper cone steers round tighter curves. A flatter cone stays calm at speed. One cone cannot do both.

## Landing
So the next time you hear that screech on a bend, you will know what it means. The cone has run out of geometry, and the steel is sliding sideways.

One solid axle. Two wheels locked together. And a slope of one in twenty. That is how a train steers with no steering at all.
