## Cold open
Picture the ropes holding your elevator snapping. What do you imagine saves you? An emergency brake that slams on as hard as it can? The real one is stranger. Nothing electric sets it off. The fall itself does. And it is built to slip.

In a typical elevator, you would fall for a fraction of a second, then stop within about a meter. So how does a falling box brake itself? Why would anyone build a brake that slips? And what about the danger it was never built to stop, an elevator that runs away upward?

## Not one cable
First, the cable you are picturing. A traction elevator rarely hangs from just one. American codes often ask for at least three steel ropes, and each must be many times stronger than its share of the load. So a snap is the rare case. But the rare case is exactly what this brake is for.

## The rope that holds nothing
At the top of the shaft, there is a wheel that lifts nothing at all. Engineers call it the governor. A thin loop of rope runs over it, down to a weighted pulley in the pit, and it is tied to your car. When you move, the rope moves with you, and the wheel spins. A faster car means a faster wheel.

In a classic governor, weights inside the wheel are held in by springs. Spin it faster, and they swing outward. At a set speed, at least fifteen percent above your elevator's normal speed, they swing out far enough to catch, and the wheel locks. Its rope stops.

But your car does not stop. It keeps falling, past a rope that is no longer moving. And that rope is tied to a lever on your car. Do you see what happens? The falling car pulls its own brake lever.

Many governors also flip a switch first, to cut the motor. But the brake itself is pure mechanics. Europe's elevator code says it must not be set off by anything electric, hydraulic or pneumatic.

## The wedge
The lever lifts a pair of steel wedges, one on each side of a guide rail. That rail runs the whole height of the shaft, and each wedge sits in a slot that narrows toward the top.

Now watch what your fall does to them. The moment a wedge touches the rail, the rail rushes upward past your falling car and drags the wedge higher into the narrowing slot. The higher it goes, the harder it squeezes. The fall is feeding its own brake.

So here is a question for you. Should a brake like this grab as hard as it can, and stop you dead? Take a second. Most of us would say yes.

## Too hard
Here is the problem. Our example elevator trips at about two meters a second. If you stopped that in two centimeters, you would slow at about ten times the pull of gravity. Every person inside takes that jolt.

The first famous safety did exactly that. In eighteen fifty-four, in New York, Elisha Otis rode a platform high in the air and had its rope cut. Springs threw hooks into teeth along the guides, and the platform stopped where it was. On a slow machine, that works. Today, a brake that grabs like that is still allowed, but only on elevators rated at sixty-three centimeters a second or less.

Faster than that? The code asks for the opposite. A brake that slows you, on average, no harder than the pull of gravity, and no softer than a fifth of it.

## The spring
So how do you stop a wedge from biting too hard? You give it a limit, and the limit is a spring. The jaws that hold the wedge are not rigid. Behind them, stacks of disc springs let the jaws open a little. The wedge climbs only so far, and from then on, the springs decide how hard the jaws squeeze.

A fixed squeeze means a fixed friction. And a fixed friction means steady slowing. So your car slides down the rail with the wedges biting. Depending on how the springs are set, you stop somewhere between twenty centimeters and a meter.

Is it gentle on the rail? Not at all. One maker's manual tells you the braking comes from metal cutting into the rail surface, from friction, and from the work of the springs. Afterward, the marks are ground smooth. The car can only be released by raising it, and a trained person must check it before it carries you again.

That is the deal. The wedge, on its own, would stop you far too hard. The spring sets the limit. Together, they turn a grab into a slide.

## The other way
But all of this only works one way. Down.

Every traction elevator you ride is balanced by a heavy counterweight on the other side of its ropes. To save energy, it usually weighs as much as the car plus about forty to fifty percent of a full load.

So when the car is empty, which side is lighter? The car. Suppose the machine's brake or its controls fail. The counterweight can drop and pull the empty car up, faster and faster, toward the top of the shaft.

And the classic safety gear? It could not help, because it only grips going down. One United States mine safety study counted at least eighteen elevators that struck the top of their shafts in just five years.

So the codes added a brake that faces up. Canada made it a rule in nineteen ninety, and Europe's code now requires protection against a car running away upward. It can be a brake that clamps the ropes, or safety gear built to grip both ways.

## Landing
So the next time elevator doors close on you, picture what is waiting for you in the dark. A thin rope you will never see, running over a wheel. Two wedges, waiting just clear of the rail. And springs, set to stop you without stopping you dead.

The safest brake is not the one that grabs the hardest. It is the one that knows how much to let go.
